Applying it · 06

Voice & copy

Plain, calm, specific, anti-hype. Numbers instead of adjectives. Slightly self-aware about being a small product. If a sentence would sound strange said out loud by a tired nurse, it is not the voice.

Principles

Five things the voice does

  • Says the number. "Fifty questions" beats "plenty of practice". "1,240 in the bank" beats "a huge question bank".
  • Names the mechanism. Not "smart learning" — "the rationale is on every question, including the ones you got right".
  • Admits the limits. Being a small product is a feature here. Overclaiming is the fastest way to sound like every other prep site.
  • Talks about the material, not the reader. "Most repeat testers lose points on prioritization", never "You keep failing prioritization".
  • Stops. One idea, one line, one action. There is no second paragraph that restates the first.

Mechanics

House style

ThingRule
Exclamation marksNone. Anywhere. Including error messages and empty states.
EmojiNone in product or marketing copy.
The product nameNursia, capital N, never all-caps, never with the full stop in running text — the full stop belongs to the logo.
The examNCLEX-RN on first mention, NCLEX after. Never "the NCLEX exam".
NumeralsFigures, not words: "50 questions", "312 items". Set in mono when they are the point.
Sentence caseHeadlines, buttons and nav are sentence case. Only mono eyebrows are uppercase.
ContractionsFine, and preferred. "It doesn't" reads calmer than "It does not".
Second personUsed for instructions ("Pick the client you would see first"), never for diagnosis of the reader.
DashesSpaced en dashes. No em dashes jammed against words.
SpellingUS English in product copy, because the exam is US.

Hard limits

Never, in any surface

  • A pass rate or score claim. No "98% pass rate", no "students improve by X points". Not in an ad, not in a footnote.
  • A fabricated testimonial or a quote attributed to someone who did not say it.
  • An invented user count or any social-proof number that is not literally counted.
  • Anything implying NCSBN endorsement or affiliation. NCLEX and NCLEX-RN are registered trademarks of the National Council of State Boards of Nursing. Nursia is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by the NCSBN, and the footer says so.
  • Medical advice. The content is exam preparation. It is never framed as clinical guidance.
  • A claim about the reader. "Failed the NCLEX?" is both unkind and, on Meta, a policy rejection. Make the claim about the material instead.
That last one is worth internalising: Meta rejects ad copy that asserts or implies something about the person seeing it — health status, failure, personal circumstance. "Most repeat testers lose points on prioritization" states a fact about a population and passes. "You keep losing points on prioritization" does not.
